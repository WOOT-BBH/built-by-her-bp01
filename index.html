The 60-second answer

A definition is a decision tool

It tells you which enquiries to take, which products to make and which requests are a distraction. A usable definition has four parts:

01

What you sell

The specific thing a person receives or pays for.

02

Who it is for

A practical description of the buyer—clear enough to find her.

03

The problem or goal

What was happening before she came to you.

04

The practical result

What she has, avoids or can do afterwards.

Accuracy beats polish. An accurate, slightly clumsy definition is far more useful than a polished, vague one.
See three examples

Local service

“I groom dogs at the owner’s home for people with nervous or elderly dogs within a 20-minute drive of [town], who find a salon visit stressful or impractical, so the dog is washed, clipped and comfortable without leaving the drive.”

Product business

“I bake gluten-free bread and rolls for independent cafés and farm shops in [county] that want a coeliac-safe menu option, so they can serve customers they currently turn away.”

Knowledge-based business

“I handle inbox, diary and supplier admin each month for small charities with no office staff, so the person running the charity is not doing admin at nine o’clock at night.”

Activity 1

Think it through

Answer from what has actually happened. If you have not traded yet, mark assumptions with an A.

Activity 2

Build your definition

Turn the evidence from Activity 1 into four clear parts.

Your working sentence

I [make / provide / sell] [what someone buys] for [who they are], who [problem or goal], so [practical result].

Vague-word check

Do any words sound good but carry little information?

Common examples: quality, bespoke, supportive, modern, affordable and tailored. Replace each one with the fact underneath it.

Activity 3

Test a real decision

A definition is useful only when it helps you choose.

Hold your definition against it. What happens?

Ask yourself

  1. Could someone correctly say what I sell and who buys it?
  2. Does anything in my current work sit outside this definition—and is that deliberate?
  3. Which part am I least sure about, and what evidence would tell me the answer?

Use AI carefully

Use AI to test clarity—not decide your direction

Write the sentence yourself first. AI does not know your customers, skills, capacity or market.

Prompt: Find the vague words
I run a business selling [what you sell] to [who it is for] in the UK. Here is my draft business definition: “[paste your sentence]”. Review it and give me three lists: (1) any word that is vague or could apply to almost any business, with a question that would help me replace it with a specific fact; (2) which of the four parts—what is sold, who it is for, the problem or goal, the practical result—is missing or weakest; (3) any claim in my sentence that I have not evidenced. Do not rewrite the sentence for me, suggest a new business direction or invent facts. List anything you had to assume.
Prompt: Test what a reader understands
Read this sentence once: “[paste your sentence]”. Without asking me anything first, tell me in your own words what you think this business sells, who you think buys it, and what problem you think it solves. Then list every point where you had to guess, and the question I would need to answer to remove each guess. Do not fill the gaps yourself or invent details.

The one thing to remember

A definition is only doing its job when it can rule something out.

If your sentence would let you say yes to every enquiry, every product idea and every opportunity, it is a description rather than a definition.
