Your identified gap

Assessment area: Website clarity

Whether your Assessment brought you here or you chose this lesson, a clear homepage helps visitors understand the business and what they can do. You can plan its words before having a website.

02

What this lesson helps you do

Draft a simple content outline for your homepage: an opening explanation, useful supporting information and a clear next step. This is a starting point for writing or briefing someone, rather than a finished website.

Allow 30 minutes: about 12 to read, 13 for brief notes and 5 to review and choose a next step.

This guide provides general information and reflection activities. Built By Her does not provide legal, financial or tax advice. Relevant official guidance and qualified support are signposted where needed.

03

The 60-second answer

Your homepage should quickly explain what you offer, who it is for and how someone can take the next step. Add the information that helps a visitor decide whether to continue, such as your location or service area, the type of work you undertake and a relevant example.

A common mistake is opening with a broad slogan or long personal story while leaving the offer unclear. Start with a plain sentence and a meaningful link or button, such as “View workshop dates” or “Ask about a project”. Support it with accurate detail and evidence you can use. Keep the page readable on a small screen and make essential information available as text. You can plan all of this in a few notes before choosing a design.

04

Work it through

Start with the question a new visitor brings

Your homepage is often the first page someone uses to understand the business. They may have followed a recommendation and know very little about you. Before asking them to explore, answer: “What can I get here, and is it relevant to me?” A visitor should not have to interpret a slogan to find the offer.

In an illustrative comparison, “Helping you reach your potential” could describe many businesses. “Small-group sewing classes for adults in York” gives a person something concrete to recognise. Start with the offer, the people it suits and any location that affects access. A remote consultant might name the business problem instead of a town. A business preparing to launch should make that stage clear rather than implying that bookings are already open.

Put decision-making information next

The opening sentence earns attention; the next section helps someone decide whether to continue. Choose details that could change that decision. A workshop visitor may need to know whether beginners can attend. A product buyer may need size and material. A service customer may need to know what work you do and how a price is agreed.

You do not need every detail on the homepage. Explain the main offer, then use clearly named links to fuller information where necessary. If you offer several services, group them in language a customer would recognise. A long menu of internal service names makes the reader do the work of deciding which one solves their problem.

Give people a truthful reason to trust the offer

Useful evidence supports a particular claim. A photograph of your own finished work can show the kind of work you do. Relevant experience can explain why you understand a problem. A genuine customer comment, used with permission, can describe someone's experience. None needs to become an unsupported promise that everyone will get the same result.

If you are new, explain your process or use an honestly labelled sample. Do not invent testimonials, qualifications or client work. If customers will work directly with you, a short introduction can help them understand who is involved. Include the part of your story that helps them assess the offer; a fuller biography can sit elsewhere.

Give the page one clear main next step

Choose the action that matches how the business works. “Ask about alterations” starts a discussion. “View available mugs” takes someone to products. “Book a place” should lead to a real booking route with important details available before commitment.

Make the destination understandable from the wording. “See what the session includes” tells someone more than “Learn more”. Several links can appear on a page, but the most useful next step should be easy to recognise. A person who is ready to enquire should not have to decide between several vague buttons or search for contact details.

Contact information and routes to relevant business details, privacy information and terms should be easy to find. Required disclosures depend on the business and transaction; check these before publication using official guidance and suitably qualified advice for an uncertain obligation.

Make the words usable before choosing a design

Use descriptive headings and short paragraphs that still explain the point. Essential information should be available as text, not only inside a photograph or graphic. Plan a suitable text description for an image that conveys information. These choices help people using small screens or assistive technology, although they are not a complete accessibility review.

What this looks like in practice

Illustrative worked example: a clothing alteration service wants a homepage that helps local people decide whether to enquire. The owner needs to see an item before agreeing the work, so she chooses an enquiry rather than immediate booking. Her completed content outline is:

1. Opening: “Clothing alterations in Leeds. I shorten trousers and adjust hems so you can keep wearing clothes you already own.”

2. Main next step: “Ask about an alteration.” This would lead to an enquiry form, with an email contact alternative.

3. Practical details: “Tell me what you would like changed. We can arrange a time for me to see the item and discuss what is possible. I agree the work and price with you before starting. Appointments are arranged in advance.”

4. Supporting evidence: “Examples of my work: trousers shortened and a skirt hem adjusted.” In this example, the owner has photographs of both pieces and permission to show them. The captions would describe the changes. A short introduction could add: “I work directly with you to discuss the changes you want.”

5. Contact and supporting information: “Tell me about the item and the change you have in mind. Include your email address so I can reply. If you have a particular deadline, mention it in your enquiry.” The form would provide relevant privacy information; business details and applicable terms would be easy to find.

6. Order and outstanding check: “Opening and enquiry link; how it works; examples and brief introduction; contact and supporting links. Confirm the appointment-location information before publication, along with the required business, privacy and terms information.”

The opening answers the visitor's immediate service and location questions. The process explains what happens before work starts, while the photographs support a specific claim about work completed. Missing publication details remain checks, not invented facts. This is a usable outline for developing the page, without needing a design or a live enquiry form today.

A maker directing visitors to available pieces

Illustrative contrast: a maker selling stoneware mugs might open with “Hand-thrown stoneware mugs made in small batches” and use “View available mugs” as the main link. Her supporting notes say: “Show photographs of my own pieces; explain that sizes and glazes vary; put each item's dimensions, material, price and delivery details on its product page.” This keeps the homepage readable while placing decision-making detail beside the item. The outline does not need every product specification repeated on the homepage.

A consultancy preparing to launch

Illustrative contrast: an experienced manager is developing a review service but has no consultancy clients yet. Her opening could be: “I'm developing an operations review for small agencies that want clearer everyday working procedures.” Her notes add a description of the proposed review, relevant experience described accurately, and “Ask about the proposed service” as the next step. She uses a labelled sample of her approach instead of invented testimonials. The page makes the planning stage clear while giving a potential enquirer something concrete to discuss.

06

Use AI carefully

AI is optional. It can organise your draft into clear sections. Leave out personal customer details, unpublished testimonials and confidential work.

Optional AI prompt · Organise your homepage outline

Turn these notes into a short homepage outline: [notes]. The offer is [offer], for [audience], at this business stage [stage]. The next step is [action]. Use everyday British English and descriptive headings. Keep supplied facts unchanged. Do not invent reviews, qualifications, prices, guarantees or availability. Mark missing information as ‘to confirm’ and suggest clear link wording.

Check every claim. AI cannot verify permissions, legal requirements, accessibility or working links from this draft.

07

The one thing to remember

A useful homepage helps a new visitor understand the offer and choose where to go next.

08

Common questions

Must I put prices on the homepage?

It depends on the offer. You can direct visitors to clear product prices or explain how a tailored quote is obtained. Required price information must be available at the appropriate point before a purchase.

What if I have no testimonials?

Use accurate information about the work, your approach and relevant experience. An honestly labelled example is more useful than an invented recommendation.

Where to go next

Keep the outline and mark the single missing fact that most affects a visitor's understanding.

For later

Optionally develop the copy, obtain image permissions, check required disclosures or ask someone to find the offer and next step. Design, publication, mobile testing and accessibility review come later. AI and further reading are outside the core 30 minutes.

Further reading and support

W3C: Writing for Web Accessibility explains useful headings, descriptive links, text alternatives and clear instructions. It can help when developing the outline into content.

GOV.UK: Online selling explains information and ordering requirements if the site will take online orders. It is not a complete review of your business's obligations.
